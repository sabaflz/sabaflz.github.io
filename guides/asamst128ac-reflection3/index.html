    01 · Workshopped in office hoursThe likely question

    Likely prompt (your GSI built this one out loud with students, so it is close to the real thing):

    "What does it mean to seek recognition as both Muslim and American in the US? Thinking through Palestinian refugeehood, what are the limits and uses of recognition by the state?"

    Two parts, one argument. Part one asks the concept: what recognition is and what it costs. Part two grounds it in the sharpest case, Palestinian refugeehood, and asks for its uses (what recognition gives) and its limits (what it withholds). Do not answer them as two separate essays, run one claim through both.

    The one-line answer: state recognition is a double-edged bargain. It is real and worth having, it brings aid, rights, and a place in the nation, but it is always partial and conditional, granted on the state's terms and only for the parts of you that serve the nation. The limit of recognition is that it never sees the whole person, only the useful part.
    MODELFull model answer for the likely prompt (about 470 words, in a handwriting voice). Adapt the opening to the real wording on the day.
    ~470 words

    To seek recognition as both Muslim and American is to ask the state for something it offers and takes back in the same motion. My argument is that state recognition is a double-edged bargain. It is real and worth having, it brings aid, rights, and a place in the nation, but it is always partial and conditional, granted on the state's terms and only for the parts of a person that serve the nation. The limit of recognition is that it never sees the whole person, only the useful part.

    Palestinian refugeehood is the clearest version of this. Hatem Bazian shows that Palestinians were recognized as refugees not through the UNHCR like everyone else, but through UNRWA, a temporary agency whose funding depends on voluntary donations, so Palestinian refugees "exist at the mercy of donor states" (25). The recognition is useful, it unlocks assistance and services. But it is defined narrowly, only people whose residence fell between June 1946 and May 1948 count (27), which writes many of the displaced out of the category. Bazian's sharpest point is that "even as the UN worked to provide support, it actually allowed Israel and its allies to define who is and who is not a Palestinian refugee, thus aiding and abetting the dispossession" (27). Recognition here is not only help, it is a tool that contains the very people it names.

    The same logic runs through Muslim-American life. GhaneaBassiri shows Muslims seeking recognition as both Muslim and American by building institutions and entering public life, yet after 9/11 they were pulled "into America's collective conscience as a security threat, a potential enemy within," and had their civil rights abused and were deported (327). They were recognized, but recognized as a danger. That is Said's point exactly: Orientalism is a system of misrecognition, in which the West keeps the authority to define Muslims and sees only the version that fits its grid.

    Bazian's "I Can't Breathe" takes the limit to its end. Black Americans are legally recognized as citizens, but the knee on George Floyd's neck marks "the difference between the human endowed with meaning and the subhuman within the colonially crafted time, space, and body" (142). Legal recognition never became recognition of full humanity.

    So seeking recognition as both Muslim and American means seeking a belonging the state hands over and withholds at once. The use of recognition is survival and a real foothold in the nation. The limit is that the state recognizes you only as far as it benefits from you, never as a whole person. That gap, between being counted and being seen, is where all three readings meet.

    Two more full essays, for the containment angle and the institutions angle, are in section 09.

    02 · The keywordWhat "state recognition" means

    Recognition is being named and counted by a power that is not your community, a nation-state, a government, or an international body like the UN, as a citizen, a refugee, a legitimate identity. That naming decides who falls under the state's care and control, and who gets benefits. It is, as the GSI put it, the premise of immigration politics: who the nation takes in.

    The uses (why people seek it)

    • Material: aid, services, rights, protection, a legal status.
    • Political: legitimacy, a voice, a seat at the table.
    • Personal: a real feeling of belonging, of being seen as part of the nation.

    The limits (what it costs)

    • Conditional: it can be narrowed or withdrawn.
    • Defining: the recognizer sets the terms, not you.
    • Selective: it recognizes only the part of you that serves the nation.
    • Control: to be recognized is to be incorporated and governed at once.
    The move that makes this an argument, not a definition: recognition and misrecognition are the same act. The state cannot see you as a full person and still manage you as a category, so it recognizes the useful part and misrecognizes the rest. Said names the misrecognition, Bazian shows it operating on Palestinian and Black bodies, GhaneaBassiri shows it flip Muslims from "both Muslim and American" into "enemy within." Commit to that single idea and the whole essay holds together.

    The course's fundamental contradiction, worth naming: yes, Muslims are in America, and also this country was not built for Muslims in America. Recognition lives inside that contradiction.

    04 · The theorySaid: recognition as misrecognition

    You know Said from Reflection 2, so here he is in one move, aimed at recognition. Orientalism is a system of knowledge that is also a system of power: the West holds the authority to describe the East, and that description is never neutral. Applied to recognition, Orientalism is how the state misrecognizes, it sees Muslims only through its own grid, so even being "recognized" means being seen as the West's idea of a Muslim, not as yourself.

    • "an accepted grid for filtering through the Orient into Western consciousness" (14). The grid is what does the misrecognizing.
    • "a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (13). Recognition flows one way, from the powerful to the named.
    • The core question: who has the authority to describe another people and decide what counts as legitimate knowledge, or legitimate identity.
    The link to make: when your other readings show Palestinians, Muslims, or Black Americans recognized only partially, Said explains why. The power that recognizes them also reserves the right to define them, so recognition and misrecognition are the same act seen from two sides.

    05 · The core caseHatem: Palestine and Settler Colonialism

    Chapter 1 of Bazian's book, partly a memoir (he grew up in the Al-Hussein refugee camp in Amman). The GSI named this the key reading. It is where "recognition by the state" becomes concrete, through the figure of the refugee.

    Refugeehood as recognition, and its price

    • A separate, lesser recognition. Palestinians fall not under the UNHCR but under UNRWA, a temporary agency created in 1950, its mandate renewed periodically and its funding from voluntary donations, so Palestinian refugees "exist at the mercy of donor states" (25).
    • The narrow definition. UNRWA counts only "persons whose normal place of residence was Palestine during the period 1 June 1946 to 15 May 1948" (27). People displaced between 1917 and 1948, and those studying abroad, are written out of the category.
    • Recognition as a tool of erasure. "even as the UN worked to provide support, it actually allowed Israel and its allies to define who is and who is not a Palestinian refugee, thus aiding and abetting the dispossession of the indigenous population" (27). This is your sharpest quote.
    • Included through omission. "Palestinians are included through structural omission" (26). Because Palestine "is not a state," Palestinians face "a separate regime when it comes to the refugee designation" (26).
    • Law as a colonial tool. "international law is the codification of the legal 'rights' and 'privileges' attained by the colonial powers during direct colonization, setting it as the measure and tool to regulate the postcolonial" (26).
    • Demographics as power. "Demographics is a central obsession of colonial enterprises and more pernicious in the settler colonial type" (27). Counting is controlling.
    The uses and limits, right here: UNRWA's recognition is a genuine use, it delivers aid and services to millions and is better than nothing. Its limit is that the same act defines Palestinians so narrowly, and on terms the colonizer helped set, that recognition becomes a way to manage the dispossession rather than end it. The Nakba, Bazian insists, "is not a singular event... Palestinians are still experiencing and living the Nakba on a daily basis" (29).

    06 · The limitHatem: I Can't Breathe

    Chapter 5. Bazian reads George Floyd's murder (May 25, 2020) as "the most painful and apt summation of the colonization effects" (139), and links it to a web of colonized and displaced bodies. For this prompt, it is the reading that shows recognition's hard limit: legal status is not the same as being recognized as human.

    • The human / subhuman line. The eight minutes and forty-six seconds are "the difference between the human endowed with the meaning contained therein and the subhuman within the colonially crafted time, space, and body" (142). Recognition decides who counts as fully human.
    • Structure, not incident. "Race and systematic and structural racism are the colonial legacies that are baked into policing, governing, education, the economy" (142). Recognition inside those institutions does not undo them.
    • The web of recognition. Floyd (descendant of enslaved Africans), the store owner (a Palestinian immigrant), Chauvin (Irish, "admitted into whiteness after the Civil War and the Chinese Exclusion Act"), officer Tou Thao (a Hmong refugee), all sit on one map of who the nation has and has not recognized as fully human (142).
    • The through-line to Palestine. Bazian ties Floyd to Iyad el-Hallaq (an autistic Palestinian killed by Israeli forces) and Alan Kurdi (the drowned Syrian toddler) (139). The knee and the refugee camp are the same logic of denied recognition.
    The move: Black Americans have been legally recognized as citizens for over 150 years, and that recognition never became protection of their personhood. Use this to show the deepest limit of recognition: the state can count you and still refuse to see you as human. confirm page w/ notes

    07 · SupplementaryGhaneaBassiri Ch 8: both Muslim and American

    "Between Experience and Politics: American Muslims and the 'New World Order,' 1989 to 2008." Abed said you may use it to guide your thinking. It gives you the Muslim-American half of the prompt in plain terms.

    • The intermediary role. "At the end of the Cold War, Islam became deeply entangled in America's most recent search for a national identity. American Muslims played an intermediary role... as individuals who are both Muslim and American" (329). That phrase is the prompt.
    • Recognized as a threat. Militant violence and the invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq "brought American Muslims into America's collective conscience as a security threat, a potential enemy within. Consequently, hundreds of American Muslims have found their civil rights abused and an untold number have been deported" (327). Recognition flipped into suspicion.
    • The surveillance state. After 9/11, Homeland Security absorbed immigration and citizenship agencies, and the PATRIOT Act gave "greater powers of surveillance and regulation," so "surveillance and the threat of terrorism became a much more routine part of Americans' lives" (328).
    • Dual authenticity. W.D. Mohammed spoke only after "establishing his authenticity as an American and a Muslim" (334), which shows the work Muslims had to do to be recognized as both at once.
    • The question in a title. GhaneaBassiri cites Yvonne Haddad's "Muslims in U.S. Politics: Recognized and Integrated, or Seduced and Abandoned?" (334 n.14). That either/or is your essay's tension in six words.

    BThesis B, recognition as containment (about 440 words)
    ~440 words

    We tend to hear recognition as a gift, the nation finally seeing you. My argument is the opposite: state recognition is mainly a technology of control. The state recognizes people in order to govern them, and Palestinian refugeehood shows the trick most clearly, because there recognition exists to manage a population, not to free it.

    Hatem Bazian makes the point almost literally. Palestinians are recognized through UNRWA, whose definition counts only "persons whose normal place of residence was Palestine during the period 1 June 1946 to 15 May 1948" (27). That sounds like bureaucratic detail, but Bazian shows its purpose: "even as the UN worked to provide support, it actually allowed Israel and its allies to define who is and who is not a Palestinian refugee, thus aiding and abetting the dispossession" (27). The act of recognizing is the act of drawing a border around who can ever return. He adds that "demographics is a central obsession of colonial enterprises" (27), because to count a people is to control them.

    Said explains why this is built in. Orientalism is "a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (13), so recognition always flows one way, from the power that defines to the people it names. There is no neutral counting. To be recognized is to accept the recognizer's terms.

    The same containment runs through Muslim-American life. GhaneaBassiri shows that after 9/11 the state recognized Muslims intensely, but as "a security threat, a potential enemy within," which is why Homeland Security absorbed the immigration agencies and the PATRIOT Act expanded "surveillance and regulation" (327 to 328). This is recognition as surveillance, being seen so you can be watched. Bazian's "I Can't Breathe" shows the endpoint: Black Americans are fully recognized as citizens, yet structural racism stays "baked into policing, governing, education, the economy" (142), because legal recognition was never meant to loosen the state's grip, only to formalize it.

    So the uses of recognition are real, aid and status and a paper that says you belong. But its deeper function is containment. The state names you to fix you in place, to decide what you can claim and how far you can go. For Palestinians that means a refugee category that forecloses return; for Muslims it means belonging that can flip to suspicion overnight. Recognition is not the end of being governed. It is how being governed begins.

    CThesis C, institutions as survival not cure (about 440 words)
    ~440 words

    One honest way to answer what it means to seek recognition as both Muslim and American is to look at how Muslims actually did it, by building institutions. My argument is that institutional recognition is real and necessary for survival, but it is a band-aid: it buys a place inside the system without changing the system that keeps denying full recognition.

    GhaneaBassiri shows that since the late 1980s American Muslims built mosques, schools, self-help organizations, and advocacy groups, and became visible in public life as "individuals who are both Muslim and American" (329). This is the optimistic reading, and it is not wrong. Institutions gave Muslims a foothold and a way to be recognized by local and federal government as a community that belongs. W.D. Mohammed could speak only after "establishing his authenticity as an American and a Muslim" (334). That feeling of recognition matters, people need institutions to survive and to be who they are.

    But recognition won this way hits its limit fast. The same chapter shows that after 9/11 all that visibility flipped, and Muslims were recognized most powerfully as "a security threat, a potential enemy within" (327). Building schools did not stop the PATRIOT Act or the deportations. Bazian's "I Can't Breathe" explains why: structural racism is "baked into policing, governing, education, the economy" (142), so recognition inside those institutions cannot undo the structure. Black Americans have been recognized as citizens for generations, and the knee stayed on the neck.

    Palestinian refugeehood makes the band-aid visible. UNRWA recognition delivers real aid, but Bazian shows it was built to manage Palestinians, since the UN let Israel "define who is and who is not a Palestinian refugee" (27). Recognition relieved suffering while leaving the dispossession in place.

    So seeking recognition as both Muslim and American through institutions is worth doing, and I would not tell anyone to stop. But it is reformist, not transformative. It wins inclusion without winning structural change, a feeling of belonging inside a system that was not built to recognize Muslims, Palestinians, or Black Americans as full people. The use of institutional recognition is survival. Its limit is that survival is not the same as justice.
