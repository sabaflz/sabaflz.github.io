    00 · Start hereWhat the exam is

    Based on the 10/8 section slides and the Fall 2020 sample exam, the midterm is a mix of multiple choice, matching, true/false, and short answer (one short paragraph each, roughly 250 words max), with a couple of extra-credit prompts. The sample was scored out of 79.

    It covers four bodies of material:

    Diouf Ch 1 African Muslims, Christian Europeans, and the transatlantic slave trade. Diouf Ch 2 Upholding the Five Pillars under slavery. Lecture + GhaneaBassiri race, religion, citizenship, and Muslim immigration. Malcolm X the "Make It Plain" documentary and his Letter from Mecca.

    01 · For the written answersThe argument engine

    The reflection-style prompt will give you a quote (likely from Malcolm X's Letter from Mecca, or an Abdul El-Sayed / Muslim-belonging quote like the sample) and ask how you would argue for Muslim belonging in the United States, using Bazian's lecture, the section slides, and Diouf. One thesis carries almost any version of it:

    Core thesis. Muslim belonging in America is not something Muslims have to prove; it is constitutive. Muslims helped make American religious, racial, and political life from the very beginning, and they built and defended that belonging under systems designed to deny it: first slavery, then racial exclusion, then surveillance. So the demand to "prove" belonging gets the history backwards.

    This works because it lets you reach into all four bodies of material in one move: Diouf for the enslaved founding presence, GhaneaBassiri for immigration and citizenship, and Malcolm X for belonging reclaimed. Three themes the slides told you the prompt will touch:

    • Islam is not "foreign" to America (the long history of African Muslims).
    • Muslim belonging under coercion (slavery, forced conversion, exclusion).
    • Religion as identity and community (from the pillars to the Nation of Islam).

    Paragraph shape to repeat: claim → named evidence → analysis → link back to the thesis. Full worked versions are in section 07.

    02 · Diouf, Chapter 1African Muslims & the slave trade

    Big idea: enslaved African Muslims did not arrive as blank slates. They brought a developed religious world, which puts Africa at the center of the story of American slavery and makes Islam a founding American presence, not a recent import.

    Islam in West Africa

    • Islam was established in West Africa from the 8th to 11th centuries, spread mostly peacefully by native traders, clerics, and rulers, not by military conquest. Africans considered it an African religion.
    • Sufi brotherhoods, above all the Qadiriyya (founded by Abdul-Qadir Gilani), linked members across ethnicity, geography, and class. Real community infrastructure.
    • West African Muslims belonged to a global Islamic world with ties to North Africa, Egypt, and the Middle East. Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage to Mecca is the famous example of that reach.

    Literacy

    • Qur'anic education encouraged literacy in Arabic and ajami (African languages written in Arabic script), plus Qur'an memorization. Schooling reached women and peasants, not only elites.
    • Why it mattered in the Americas: religious continuity, community formation, communication, the pursuit of freedom, and resistance. (This is the sample exam's short-answer #9.)

    Slavery: African vs. American systems

    African contexts

    • Multiple forms of slavery; varied by region, period, religion
    • Captives mostly from warfare
    • Religion (not race) shaped status; Muslims could be enslaved or slaveholders
    • Manumission possible; status could change; Islamic law barred enslaving freeborn Muslims and encouraged freeing slaves

    American contexts

    • Racialized slavery: race/color linked to status
    • Captives bought/kidnapped for a plantation economy
    • Hereditary (a child inherited the mother's status), making Blackness itself associated with permanent enslavement
    • Manumission far more restricted; Christian conversion was the justification for enslavement, not an exit

    This is sample short-answer #1: "Name THREE points of difference." Any three of the rows above will do.

    How Muslims ended up enslaved anyway

    • Wars, jihads, and the Atlantic trade overrode the protections. Muslims were sometimes sold by other Muslims by declaring them "lax" or kafir.
    • Complicated rebellion: Islamic movements could oppose enslaving Muslims while still enslaving non-Muslims; religious wars produced both liberation and new captives (e.g., the Oyo conflict of 1817, where enslaved people were promised freedom for joining the jihad; Muslim forces later founded the Ilorin Emirate while prisoners were shipped to the Americas). Diouf presents rebellion, warfare, slavery, and Islam as interconnected, not separate.
    • Spanish authorities repeatedly tried to restrict African Muslim arrivals, fearing they would incite other enslaved people to rebellion. The first African slave revolt in the Americas was led by Muslim Wolof in Hispaniola, 1522.
    • Scale: about 12.5 million Africans were deported overall; exact Muslim numbers are unknowable, which Diouf states plainly.

    03 · Diouf, Chapter 2Upholding the Five Pillars

    Big idea: Diouf organizes the chapter around the Five Pillars to show Muslim identity persisting under conditions built to suppress it. That persistence is itself community-building and resistance.

    • Backdrop: forced Christianization. Catholics mandated baptism (French Code Noir, 1685) and achieved conversions, even superficial ones, through harsh punishment; Catholicism became the state religion of the Spanish colonies. Protestants were at first reluctant to convert enslaved people, fearing a Christian could not be kept enslaved, then reversed after the Great Awakening.
    • 1. Shahada (profession of faith). Held three ways: affirming it (Ayuba Suleyman Diallo's "Allah. Muhammad."), refusing to convert, and pseudoconversion (professing Christianity while staying Muslim). Omar ibn Said filled Arabic manuscripts with praise of God; Bilali Mohamed died reciting the shahada; Muhammad Kaba wrote a 50-page Arabic treatise on prayer years after baptism.
    • 2. Salat (prayer). In secret (Diallo in the woods), in public (Yarrow Mamout in Maryland's streets), and in groups (Friday prayer in Brazil in makeshift mosques, driven into hiding after the 1835 revolt). Many adapted to three or two prayers a day. The 100-bead tasbih (vs. the usual 33) ties to Sufi dhikr and points to trained practitioners.
    • 3. Zakah (alms). Kept alive through the saraka rice cakes that Muslim women in the Georgia Sea Islands made from rice and sugar saved out of their rations. The word comes from Arabic sadaqah; it is the one pillar practice the record ties specifically to women, and food/ritual/generosity became ways to pass Islam to children.
    • 4. Sawm (fasting). They fasted through Ramadan even though their conditions would have excused them, so it was a choice.
    • 5. Hajj (pilgrimage). Nearly impossible, but connections to the wider Muslim world remained; the ring shout may echo the tawaf (from Arabic sha'wt, one circuit of the Kaaba). An 1831 letter circulating in Jamaica hints at Islamic networks reaching across the Atlantic.
    • Pseudo-conversion was a method of resistance, not surrender (sample T/F #10 = True).

    04 · Bazian lecture + GhaneaBassiriRace, religion & citizenship

    Big idea: at the turn of the 20th century, race and religion became intertwined in deciding who could enter the US and who counted as "American." Muslim immigrants navigated that by emphasizing ethnicity or "whiteness" to gain citizenship, and the belonging that began with enslaved Muslims continues through immigration and Black Muslim movements.

    Core concepts (prime matching targets)

    • Sacred Economy: the commonly held idea that Christianity and commerce go hand in hand; used to justify slavery and colonial expansion. GhaneaBassiri
    • Progress: along with race and religion, the concept that let Protestants define America as white and Protestant, maintaining cultural and sociopolitical hegemony.
    • Religion (as a category): was overshadowed at the turn of the 20th century by race and ethnicity as Muslim immigrants shaped a sense of national belonging in order to achieve citizenship.
    • De-negrofication: the process of separating African Muslims from the general enslaved Black population, recasting them as "Moorish," "Arab," or noble/exotic, to justify exceptional treatment. Many African Muslims complied with it, hoping for better treatment or passage back to Africa (sample T/F #8 = True). Two examples: Abdul Rahman Ibrahima ("the Prince," framed as a Moor, his story publicized, freed by the President's order and sent to Liberia) and Ayuba Suleyman Diallo / Job Ben Solomon (elevated as a noble, learned Muslim, taken to England, celebrated, portrait painted, returned to Africa). confirm w/ notes
    • Process of Rendition: where the US covertly sends people to be interrogated and tortured abroad, in countries with weaker human-rights rules. Shows the state treating Muslims as security threats.
    • "Passing": reveals that race, religion, and immigration were linked: who got to be considered "white" or "American" was a moving line Muslim immigrants had to negotiate.

    Exclusion legislation (and the through-line to today)

    • Chinese Exclusion Act (1882): exclusionary legislation that set a precedent for the 2017 Muslim Ban.
    • Asiatic Barred Zone (1917 Immigration Act): coupled with literacy tests, it limited immigration of Muslims (among others) from Asia.
    • National-origins quotas (1924) further restricted entry; the 1965 Hart-Celler Act abolished those quotas and reopened immigration.

    Muslim migration waves confirm w/ notes

    Sample short-answer #4 asks for THREE waves with approximate dates and what drove each. Standard course framing:

    1. First wave, ~1875–1924. From the Levant (Greater Syria: today's Lebanon, Syria, Palestine, Jordan), Ottoman subjects, mostly unskilled laborers and peddlers, drawn by economic opportunity. Curtailed by the 1917 Asiatic Barred Zone and the 1924 national-origins quotas.
    2. Second wave, ~post-WWII to 1965. Students, professionals, and elites, driven by decolonization, political upheaval, and the pull of US education.
    3. Third wave, post-1965 (Hart-Celler) through the 1990s. Professionals and STEM experts, students, and family reunification from South Asia, the Middle East, and the broader Muslim world. Intermarriage and professional migration built lasting communities. Refugees form a related but separate stream.

    Levant = birthplace of the majority of the first wave (1875–1912). Intermarriage + the need for STEM experts = what built Muslim communities from the 1960s–1990s. Both are sample matching answers.

    Six reasons Muslims migrate (name three) confirm w/ notes

    Economic opportunity / labor demand; education (students); escaping political persecution or instability; religious freedom; family reunification; refuge from war or colonial violence.

    Immigrant vs. refugee experience (name three differences)

    • Choice vs. force: immigrants generally choose to move for opportunity; refugees are forced out by war, persecution, or disaster.
    • Legal category: "refugee" is a highly politicized category contingent on US foreign-policy objectives (sample matching answer), so who counts as a refugee shifts with politics; immigrants move through a different, more planned legal track.
    • Resources and preparation: immigrants often arrive with savings, skills, or family ties and plans; refugees often arrive with little, after trauma and loss, dependent on resettlement.
    • Relationship to "home": immigrants may return or move freely; refugees often cannot safely return.

    Vertical vs. horizontal barriers to the spread of Islam confirm w/ notes

    Sample short-answer #3 asks for THREE of each. Framing: vertical = passing Islam down through generations; horizontal = spreading it across a population in the same generation.

    Vertical (generational) barriers

    • Children inherited enslaved status and were Christianized
    • Families separated by sale, breaking transmission
    • Loss of Arabic literacy; no mosques or Qur'anic schools to teach the next generation

    Horizontal (same-generation) barriers

    • Dispersal across distant plantations
    • Ethnic and linguistic diversity among the enslaved
    • Forced conversion, surveillance, and bans on gathering

    05 · "Make It Plain" + Letter from MeccaMalcolm X

    The exam tests themes from the documentary and the letter. You will not need to have read the letter closely; you need the arc of his life and what Islam meant at each stage.

    The arc, stage by stage

    • Upbringing. Father Earl Little followed Marcus Garvey (UNIA, Black nationalism, "Back to Africa"). The family faced KKK / white-supremacist violence; the father's death and the family's separation by state authorities shaped Malcolm's distrust of American institutions.
    • Prison & transformation. After criminal activity, Malcolm educates himself intensively (history, religion, philosophy, literature), sharpens his skills in prison debates, and encounters the teachings of Elijah Muhammad. Leaves prison transformed.
    • Nation of Islam. Emphasizes Black racial pride, Black self-sufficiency, separation from white society, and rejection of white supremacy. Malcolm becomes its most powerful speaker and builds Black-owned institutions (mosques, businesses, restaurants, newspapers).
    • On white supremacy. He rejects integration alone as a fix, argues Black people should control their own communities, and frames racism as a structural system, not just individual prejudice. Key discussion hook: if racial separation is produced by white supremacy, Black separation can be a form of resistance rather than another racism, and he distinguishes resistance from liberation.
    • Elijah Muhammad: mentor then conflict. Begins as mentor/father figure; Malcolm becomes disillusioned, questions NOI leadership and its response to violence ("accused them of converting the Nation of Islam into a criminal organization"). Leaves the Nation of Islam in 1964.
    • Changing politics. Early Malcolm: NOI, Black separatism, religious transformation, strong opposition to integration. Later Malcolm: leaves NOI, starts new organizations, becomes more politically active, connects Black liberation to African and Latin American liberation, and urges Black Americans to see themselves as part of a global majority rather than a US minority.

    Islam in his life (six framings)

    • Pathway to transformation (discipline, education, self-reform in prison).
    • Racial pride (Black identity through dignity, self-determination, resistance to white supremacy).
    • Community-building (NOI institutions: economic and communal independence).
    • Alternative to an American identity imposed by slavery, becoming "Malcolm X," then el-Hajj Malik el-Shabazz after Mecca, rejects the surname inherited through slavery and reclaims a connection to a lost African past.
    • Political education (conversion grows into a structural critique of racial power; organize rather than depend on white institutions).
    • Evolves internationally after leaving the NOI, connecting Black American struggles to global liberation.

    Letter from Mecca (April 1964)

    • He performs the hajj: seven circuits of the Ka'ba (tawaf), drinks from Zamzam, runs between Safa and Marwah, prays at Mina and Arafat. The pillar enslaved Muslims could only re-create, now performed.
    • He witnesses brotherhood among people of all colors ("from blue-eyed blonds to black-skinned Africans") and calls Islam "the one religion that erases from its society the race problem."
    • His central turn: from the Oneness of God to the Oneness of Man. He publicly revises his views and predicts that the younger white generation in colleges and universities may turn toward truth.
    • Signs it El-Hajj Malik El-Shabazz.

    "Chickens coming home to roost"

    Malcolm's framing (made about the JFK assassination) that the violence the US commits abroad returns to be felt at home. Connects US violence overseas with violence experienced inside the country. Good for a question linking foreign and domestic violence.

    Hatem Bazian's lecture reflections

    • A Malcolm X speech happened in a UC Berkeley hall, "history happening where we are."
    • The FBI and CIA kept thousands of files on Black leaders (MLK, Malcolm X), treating them as national-security threats. The state policed Black Muslim belonging because it was powerful.
    • Footage of Muhammad Ali shows how much his public stance shifted over his life (and his draft refusal made Black Muslim identity highly visible).

    07 · Practice exam (with answers)Test yourself

    These are the actual questions from the Fall 2020 sample, with worked answers. Try each before you open it.

    Multiple choice

    MC1Which was NOT a major mode of conversion in the expansion of Islam in Sub-Saharan Africa? (A) Military force (B) Marriage (C) Trade and commerce (D) Sufism
    A · Military force

    Islam spread into Sub-Saharan Africa mostly peacefully, through trade, marriage, and Sufi networks, not conquest.

    MC2What year marked the beginning of the systematic transportation of slaves to the New World? (A) 1526 (B) 1492 (C) 1510 (D) 1519
    Likely A · 1526

    Diouf dates the first deportations to 1501, with large-scale, systematic transport developing across the 1510s–1520s. Among these options the intended answer is almost certainly A (1526), but this is the one MCQ worth checking against your lecture notes. confirm w/ notes

    MC3This branch of Islam believes the Prophet's succession should be based on bloodline: (A) Nation of Islam (B) Shi'a (C) ISIS (D) Sunni
    B · Shi'a

    Shi'a Islam holds that leadership should pass through the Prophet's bloodline (through Ali and his descendants). Sunni Islam does not.

    MC4It was hard for Muslim slaves to uphold dietary restrictions on plantations because: (A) slaveowners served pork, cheaper than other meats (B) no access to wine before dinner (C) slaveowners served shrimp (D) couldn't eat a meal with each prayer
    A · Pork

    Pork is forbidden in Islam, and slaveowners regularly served it because it was cheap. (Wine is also forbidden, not required; shrimp is generally permitted, so B and C are wrong.)

    MC5Which was NOT a role the Prophet Muhammad served for the community? (A) Commander in Chief (B) Head of State (C) Incarnation of God (D) Adjudicator
    C · Incarnation of God

    Islam is strictly monotheistic; Muhammad is a prophet, never divine. He was a political, military, and judicial leader.

    True / False

    TF1African and Arab traders in West Africa were the primary beneficiaries of the trans-Atlantic slave trade.
    False

    Europeans and Americans were the primary beneficiaries. Africans participated, but the wealth and plantation economy accrued to the colonial powers.

    TF2Western and Central Africans were stripped of their clothes, the first of countless violations imposed as they boarded slave ships.
    True
    TF3Slavery in West Africa under Islam did not permit slaves to buy their freedom.
    False

    Islamic law provided several routes to freedom: ransom, self-redemption, exchange, manumission.

    TF4Spain continuing to pass anti-Muslim migration legislation shows previous exclusionary measures had not succeeded in stopping the arrival of Muslim slaves.
    True

    Repeated legislation is evidence the earlier bans weren't working, Muslims kept arriving.

    TF5In West Africa, the spread of literacy and Islam went hand in hand.
    True
    TF6Schools in West Africa were not open to girls and non-Muslims; they were exclusively for boys studying the religious sciences.
    False

    Girls (~20%) and non-Muslims attended Qur'anic schools; literacy reached women and peasants.

    TF7Muslim slaves were accused of cooperating with Native Americans because, unlike Native Americans, many had experience using horses in warfare.
    True

    Colonists feared Muslim military/cavalry experience and a Muslim–Native alliance.

    TF8Many African Muslims complied with de-negrofication in hopes of better treatment or possibly passage back to Africa.
    True

    See Abdul Rahman Ibrahima and Ayuba Diallo, being recast as "Moorish"/noble opened doors.

    TF9France was keen on sending all its African Muslim slaves to Europe first to baptize them before sending them to its American colonies.
    False
    TF10Pseudo-conversion was a method of resistance among enslaved communities in the New World.
    True

    08 · Short answer & reflectionModel answers

    Patterns, not scripts, the wording is yours on the day. Each is kept to one tight paragraph.

    SA1THREE differences between the European and Islamic systems of slavery.
    ~90 wordsIn the Islamic/African system, enslavement was tied to religion and circumstance rather than race, captives came mainly from warfare, and freedom was reachable through manumission, ransom, or self-redemption, so status could change. The European/American system was racialized: slavery was linked to Blackness, made hereditary so a child inherited the mother's status, and offered few routes out, with Christian conversion used to justify bondage rather than end it. A third contrast is permanence: African slavery could be temporary or mobile, while American slavery hardened into a permanent, inheritable caste.
    SA2Define "de-negrofication" and give TWO examples.
    ~85 wordsDe-negrofication is the process of separating African Muslims from the general enslaved Black population by recasting them as "Moorish," "Arab," or noble, so they could be treated as exceptions to racial slavery; many Muslims complied, hoping for better treatment or passage home. Abdul Rahman Ibrahima was publicized as a "Moorish prince," freed by the President's order, and sent to Liberia. Ayuba Suleyman Diallo (Job Ben Solomon) was elevated as a learned, noble Muslim, taken to England, celebrated, and returned to Africa.
    SA3THREE vertical and THREE horizontal barriers to the spread of Islam in the US.
    ~80 wordsVertical barriers blocked passing Islam down through generations: children inherited enslaved status and were Christianized, families were separated by sale, and Arabic literacy and institutions (mosques, Qur'anic schools) were lost. Horizontal barriers blocked spreading it across a generation: enslaved people were dispersed across distant plantations, they came from many ethnic and linguistic groups, and forced conversion, surveillance, and bans on gathering prevented communal practice. confirm w/ notes
    SA4Identify THREE Muslim migration waves (approx. dates), and what drove each.
    ~95 wordsFirst wave (~1875–1924): mostly unskilled laborers and peddlers from the Levant (Greater Syria), drawn by economic opportunity and ended by the 1917 Asiatic Barred Zone and 1924 quotas. Second wave (post-WWII to 1965): students, professionals, and elites, driven by decolonization, political upheaval, and US education. Third wave (post-1965 Hart-Celler through the 1990s): professionals and STEM experts, students, and family reunification from South Asia and the Middle East, with intermarriage building lasting communities. confirm w/ notes
    SA5THREE of the six reasons Muslims migrate to the US.
    ~45 wordsEconomic opportunity and labor demand; education (coming as students); and escaping political persecution or instability. (Others: religious freedom, family reunification, and refuge from war or colonial violence.) confirm w/ notes
    SA6THREE differences between the Muslim immigrant and refugee experiences.
    ~80 wordsFirst, choice versus force: immigrants generally choose to move for opportunity, while refugees are forced out by war or persecution. Second, legal category: "refugee" is a politicized status contingent on US foreign-policy objectives, so who qualifies shifts with politics, whereas immigrants move through a more planned track. Third, resources and return: immigrants often arrive with savings, skills, and the option to return, while refugees often arrive with little, after trauma, and cannot safely go back.
    SA7Why did Abdulrahman's former master make his freedom conditional on departure from the US?
    ~70 wordsA freed Black Muslim who stayed would be a living contradiction of slavery's logic and could unsettle the racial order, so requiring Abdulrahman to leave the country removed that threat. It also fit the colonization agenda of sending freed Black people to Africa (Liberia). Freeing him but not his children kept the master's property and the hereditary system intact while letting him appear benevolent.
    SA9What role did literacy play for enslaved Muslims? List THREE ways they preserved it.
    ~85 wordsLiteracy let Islam survive as more than memory: it preserved religious knowledge, connected scattered believers, and fed resistance, which is also why it frightened enslavers. They preserved it by writing in Arabic (Omar ibn Said's manuscripts; Muhammad Kaba's treatise on prayer), by reproducing the Qur'an from memory (Ayuba Diallo), and by using writing to communicate across distances (the Arabic letter circulating in 1831 Jamaica), keeping a textual, transmissible faith alive.
    SA10FOUR findings relating to gender, ethnicity, linguistic, and immigration patterns.
    ~90 wordsGender: the saraka charity cakes are the one pillar practice tied specifically to women, yet women are otherwise largely absent from the record. Ethnicity: the enslaved came from many groups (Wolof, Fulani, Mandingo), and Islam offered transethnic solidarity across them. Linguistic: literacy spanned Arabic and ajami (African languages in Arabic script). Immigration: later Muslim arrival was shaped by race and policy, the Levantine first wave, exclusion laws, and post-1965 professional migration, so who could belong was defined by race as much as faith.

    Extra credit

    EC1An example where gender/sexism/heteropatriarchy is erased in the historical framing of Islam in America.
    ~70 wordsThe record centers men, scholars, imams, and writers like Omar ibn Said or Bilali, while women appear almost only through the saraka cakes, even though women sustained religious transmission in the household. Framing the story around male literacy and leadership erases the domestic, women-carried labor that actually passed Islam to the next generation, treating heteropatriarchal authority as the whole of "Muslim history."
    EC2Why study the trans-Atlantic African slave trade when studying the histories of Muslims in America?
    ~70 wordsBecause it relocates the origin of Islam in America from recent immigration to the founding violence of the nation. Enslaved African Muslims were among the earliest Muslims here, which proves Islam is not foreign to America and shows belonging being built and defended under coercion. Ignoring the slave trade lets the false story of Islam as a recent, foreign arrival stand.

    REFReveal the full model reflection
    The claim that Muslims must prove they belong in America gets the history backwards. Belonging is not a door Muslims are asking to be let through; it is something they have been building here for five centuries, usually under conditions designed to deny it. Read against the course, the demand to "belong" describes a history that is already American.

    Diouf's first chapter makes this concrete. African Muslims were among the earliest people forced into the Americas, and they did not arrive empty. They came literate in Arabic and ajami, formed by Qur'anic schooling that reached women and peasants, and connected through Sufi orders like the Qadiriyya that linked believers across ethnicity. That reframes slavery itself: Africa sits at the center of the American story, and Islam is a founding presence, not a recent import. The Wolof-led revolt in Hispaniola in 1522, the first African slave revolt in the Americas, shows Muslims shaping the hemisphere from the start.

    Her second chapter shows that belonging surviving deliberate erasure. Under systems built to suppress it, Muslims kept the pillars, affirming the shahada, praying in secret and in the open, fasting through a Ramadan the Qur'an would have excused, and turning charity into the saraka cakes that women passed to their children. Some refused baptism; others professed Christianity while remaining Muslim. Belonging here is persistence, not comfort.

    Bazian's lecture and GhaneaBassiri carry it forward. At the turn of the twentieth century, race and religion together decided who could enter and who counted as American, and Muslim immigrants had to navigate exclusion laws like the Asiatic Barred Zone and emphasize ethnicity to gain citizenship. The Nation of Islam later rebuilt a collective identity for people whose names and histories slavery had stripped, and Malcolm X internationalized that struggle, finding at Mecca the raceless brotherhood American life had denied him.

    Taken together, these histories answer the demand that Muslims prove they belong. Belonging is not late legal permission; it is constitutive. Muslims have helped make American religious, racial, and political life since 1501, usually under conditions meant to deny them, which is exactly why the insistence that they are outsiders rings so false.
