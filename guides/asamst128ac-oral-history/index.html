    00 · Start hereWhat the project is

    You interview one person who identifies as Muslim, record it, then write a paper of at least 4 full double-spaced pages (title page, works cited, and appendix do not count). The paper has to do three things, and the rubric scores each of them:

    • Document what your interviewee shared, in their own voice, with real quotes.
    • Connect it to the course, at least three distinct readings, lectures, or videos, each tied to a specific moment in the interview.
    • Argue what this one life reveals about Muslim lives and communities, with a clear thesis.
    The one thing this kit cannot do for you, and why that is fine. This paper runs on a real interview with a real person. The quotes, the recording, and the transcript all have to be genuine, and the rubric fails a fictional interviewee outright. So I am not writing you an essay here. I am setting up everything around the interview so that once you have it recorded, the paper comes together fast. When you have your transcript, bring it back and we will build the paper from your person's actual words.

    02 · The scoring mapThe rubric, decoded

    100 points across seven criteria. Here is what "Exemplary" actually asks for, and the move that gets you there.

    CriterionWhat the top score needs, and how to hit it
    Interview process and ethics 10Consent and any anonymity request documented; a line or two on the setting, the length, and how you chose this person; and evidence you asked thoughtful follow-up questions live, not just read off a list. Covered by sections 03 and 04.
    Interviewee's story and findings 20A vivid, specific portrait drawn from the themes they raised, with three or more direct quotes, each introduced, explained, and cited. Their voice comes through, not your paraphrase.
    Connection to course material 20Three or more distinct course sources, cited by author or title and page or timestamp, each tied to a specific moment in the interview, explaining how they confirm, complicate, or challenge each other. Covered by section 05.
    Analysis and argument 20A clear thesis about what this story reveals. Treats your interviewee as one voice, not a stand-in for all Muslims. Reflects on what oral history shows that texts alone cannot. Covered by section 06.
    Organization and clarity 10Intro sets up the interviewee and the thesis, every paragraph has one point, conclusion returns to the argument. Do not follow the interview's question order, that is explicitly a lower score.
    Citations and mechanics 10One consistent style (MLA is simplest), the interview cited in the text, a complete works cited, few errors, correct font and spacing. Covered by section 07.
    Appendix 10The recording (audio or video) and 1 to 2 pages of accurately transcribed excerpts. Both, or you lose half. Covered by section 07.

    The easy points are ethics, citations, and the appendix, 30 points for doing the process correctly. Do not leave those on the table.

    03 · Before the interviewWho to ask, and consent

    Choosing your person

    • They must identify as Muslim. A classmate, a friend, a relative, someone from the MSA or a local mosque, a neighbor, a coworker. One person only.
    • Pick someone who will talk openly and has some texture to their story: a migration or conversion arc, a relationship to a community, or a clear experience of belonging or exclusion. That gives you something to argue about.
    • Practical beats perfect. Someone who will actually sit down with you this week is worth more than the ideal person who keeps rescheduling.

    Consent, anonymity, and the log

    This is 10 easy points and it protects your interviewee. Do all of it.

    • Tell them plainly what it is for: a class paper, it will be recorded, you will transcribe and quote parts of it, and it goes to your instructor.
    • Offer anonymity. Ask if they want a pseudonym. If yes, use a fake name everywhere in the paper and note that you did.
    • Record the consent. At the very start of the recording, say the date and have them say out loud that they agree to be recorded and quoted. That recorded "yes" is your documentation.
    • Keep an interview log for the appendix: date, location or platform, length, how consent was given, and one line on how you chose them.

    The exact spoken consent script is in the printable Bring to the interview section, so you can read it straight off your phone.

    04 · In the roomHow to run a good interview

    Straight from the UCLA Center for Oral History guidelines Abed attached. The whole art is this: it is about the narrator, not you, so you listen and they talk.

    The core stance

    • Sustained listening. The narrator does almost all the talking; you guide.
    • Structure it chronologically, it gives their story depth and keeps it from rambling.
    • Ask for their own experience before their analysis. Story first, opinions second.

    How to ask

    • Open each topic wide: "Tell me about...", "Can you walk me through...", "What was that like?"
    • Use yes/no questions only to clarify a detail, sparingly.
    • No leading or either/or questions, they foreclose their real answer.
    • One concise question at a time.

    The things that quietly raise your grade

    • Follow-ups are graded. When something is interesting, stay there: "Can you say more about that?", "Do you have a specific memory of it?", "How did that feel at the time?" That is the difference between Exemplary and Developing on the first criterion.
    • Do not interrupt, and do not fear silence. If they pause to think, let them. The best material comes after the pause.
    • Signal turns so it does not feel choppy: "We have been talking about your childhood, I would love to move to when you came to the US."
    • Save the heavy questions. Start light, build trust, and ask about harder experiences once they are warmed up.
    • Challenge gently only if needed, and never as yourself: "Some critics have argued..., how do you see it?"

    Bring thisThe interview script

    Everything to read off your phone during the interview: the consent script, then the questions in order. Abed's question set, resequenced chronologically with warm questions first and open-ended phrasing. You will not ask all of these, follow the person.

    Consent script, read this first, with the recording already running:

    "Today is [date]. Thanks again for doing this. This is for a class paper on Muslim life in America. I am recording us, and I will transcribe and quote parts of it for my instructor. You can skip any question or stop anytime, and if you would like, I can use a different name for you in the paper. Is it okay with you that I record this and quote you?" (Wait for a clear spoken "yes.")

    Warm-up and background

    1. To start, where were you born, and where do you call home now? Follow-up: Can you tell me about the places you have called home over your life?
    2. How would you describe the community and the environment you grew up in?
    3. What does the idea of home mean to you, and has that meaning changed over time?

    Migration and family journey

    1. If it applies, can you share the story of how you or your family came to the United States? Follow-up: What circumstances shaped that journey?
    2. How have your family, your cultural background, or that migration history shaped your relationship with Islam?

    Coming to Islam and religious identity

    1. Is there a story about how you came to Islam? Were you born into a Muslim family, or did you come to it later? Follow-up: What was that like for you?
    2. What are some of your earliest memories of Islam? How was religion practiced or expressed in your household?
    3. How has your relationship with Islam changed throughout your life?
    4. Are there particular traditions or practices that hold personal significance for you? Follow-up: Can you walk me through one of them?

    Community and belonging

    1. Have you found a sense of community or belonging within Muslim spaces in America? What have those spaces looked like for you?
    2. How would you describe the diversity of the Muslim community where you live? Follow-up: Do you feel a shared sense of identity across different racial, ethnic, and national backgrounds?

    Being Muslim in America (the heavier questions, ask once warmed up)

    1. What has your experience been like being Muslim in the United States? Follow-up: In your experience, how have race, ethnicity, and culture intersected with your Muslim identity?
    2. Have there been moments when you felt particularly conscious of your Muslim identity? Follow-up: Can you tell me about a specific one?
    3. How have broader political or historical events shaped your experience of being Muslim in America?
    4. Do you feel that mainstream representations of Muslims reflect your own experience? What do you think is missing?

    Reflection and close

    1. What do you wish more people understood about Muslims and Muslim life in America?
    2. Is there anything about your life, faith, or experience I have not asked about that you would like to share?

    Close by thanking them, confirming the pseudonym choice, and asking if there is anything they said that they would rather you not quote.

    05 · 20 pointsConnecting to the course

    The rubric wants three or more distinct course sources, each tied to a specific moment in your interview, with a page number or timestamp, and a sentence on whether the source confirms, complicates, or challenges what your person said. Here is a map, so whatever themes come up, you have a reading ready. Pick the three that actually fit your interview.

    If your person talks about...Reach for this source, and the link to make
    Their family's migration, citizenship, fitting in, or changing a nameGhaneaBassiri Ch 4 race, religion, and citizenship around 1900; "religious identities became secondary." Does your person's story confirm that belonging still runs through ethnicity, or challenge it?
    Mosques, Sunday school, MSA, building Muslim communityGhaneaBassiri Ch 7 post-1965 institution building, the local mosque as the central institution. Is their community one of these institutions in action?
    Feeling stereotyped, misrepresented, "what is missing" in the mediaSaid, Orientalism the "accepted grid for filtering" Muslims into Western consciousness, and the everyday, security, and gendered forms. Your person naming what is missing is them pushing back on the grid.
    Surveillance, policing, post-9/11, feeling watched or unsafe, anti-BlacknessBazian, "I Can't Breathe" Islamophobia, policing, and the securitized Muslim body. Pair with GhaneaBassiri's "process of rendition" if it fits. pull exact page from the chapter
    Palestine, solidarity, feeling their politics shape how they are seenBazian, "Palestine and Settler Colonialism" how Palestine and settler-colonial framing shape Muslim and Arab belonging in the US. pull exact page from the chapter
    Daily faith practice, prayer, the pillars, keeping faith under pressureDiouf, Servants of Allah the long continuity of Muslim practice in America, or Ch 7's "pure" vs "cultural" Islam debate.
    Conversion, Black Muslim identity, the Nation of IslamMalcolm X "Make It Plain" and the Letter from Mecca, plus Ch 7 on Warith Deen Mohammed. How does their path echo or differ from that arc?
    The pattern for each connection, in three sentences:

    1. Quote the interview moment. 2. Bring in the source: "This echoes GhaneaBassiri, who argues... (page)." 3. Say what the pairing does: "Her story confirms his point, but complicates it, because..." That third sentence, the confirm/complicate/challenge move, is what separates a 20 from a 14.

    06 · 40 pointsWriting the paper

    Four full pages, double-spaced, organized as an argument, not a transcript walkthrough. The single biggest trap, named right in the rubric, is letting the paper follow your interview question order. Build it around your thesis instead.

    Finding your thesis

    After you transcribe, read your quotes and ask: what does this one life show about Muslim life in America that a textbook alone would miss? Your thesis is the answer. It should be specific to your person and make a claim, not just a topic. Three shapes that tend to work (fill them with your real material):

    • "[Name]'s story shows that belonging for Muslims in America is built through [community / everyday practice / institutions] rather than granted, which [confirms / complicates] GhaneaBassiri's account of..."
    • "[Name]'s frustration with how Muslims are represented reveals that Said's Orientalist 'grid' still operates in daily life, but also that [he/she] resists it by..."
    • "The diversity [Name] describes in [his/her] own community challenges the idea of a single 'Muslim American' experience, and shows why one voice cannot stand in for all."

    A structure that fits 4-plus pages

    Intro (half a page)

    Introduce your interviewee in a sentence or two (pseudonym if they asked), name the setting briefly, and state your thesis.

    Body (about 3 pages)

    Three or four paragraphs, each making one point toward the thesis. In each: introduce a quote, give it, explain it, then tie in a course source with the confirm/complicate move.

    The analysis move

    Somewhere in the second half, step back: name that this is one voice, not all Muslims, and say what hearing it live showed you that the readings alone could not.

    Conclusion (half a page)

    Return to the thesis with what the story added. No new evidence, just the payoff.

    Quote handling (worth a lot here)

    Every quote gets the same three steps: introduce ("When I asked about her first years in Ohio, she said..."), quote, then explain why it matters. Never drop a quote and move on, that is the "reads like a list of Q and A answers" trap. Use at least three.

    07 · 20 pointsAppendix and citations

    The appendix (do not skip any piece)

    • Interview log: date, location or platform, length, how consent was given, how you chose them. A short block is fine.
    • The recording: audio or video. A phone voice memo or a Zoom recording both count. Submit the file or a link.
    • Transcript: 1 to 2 pages of the excerpts you quote or draw on, transcribed accurately, with timestamps. You do not transcribe the whole interview, just these.
    Transcribing faster: record, then run the audio through a free tool (Otter, the voice-typing in Google Docs, or YouTube auto-captions on an unlisted upload) to get a rough draft, then fix it by ear against the recording for the parts you will quote. Clean up only the excerpts you need.

    Citations, pick one style and stay in it

    • MLA is the least fuss. Cite the interview in the text, for example (Rahman, personal interview, 25 Nov. 2026), and list it in Works Cited.
    • Cite each reading by author and page, each video by title and timestamp.
    • Works Cited lists every course text and video you used, any outside source, and the interview itself. Nothing you cited in the body is allowed to be missing from it.
