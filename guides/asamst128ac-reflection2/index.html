    00 · Start hereWhat this reflection is

    Written in discussion during the week of Oct 26. Handwritten, about 30 minutes, 400 to 500 words. The prompt is handed out on the spot, so you learn the argument, not a script. The whole game is one argument carried all the way through.

    What the GSI wants, in his words: "a sustained elaboration of what you argue, rather than multiple arguments at once. Commit to a point and flesh out your thinking from there." So do not list three ideas. Pick one claim and dig.

    The frame to answer

    What to make of institutionalizing Islam in a time where assimilation is still a primary factor of survival in the US. The two processes to hold in your head:

    • Said Orientalism's impact on Western and US knowledge production, how the West produces authoritative knowledge about Muslims.
    • GhaneaBassiri the institutionalization of Islamic learning in the US, how Muslims built mosques, schools, and organizations to define themselves.

    His real question: do these run at the same time? (Yes.) And what does it mean to build Islamic institutions when assimilation is still the price of acceptance?

    Primary sources: GhaneaBassiri Ch 7 (primary), Ch 4 (helps), Said's Orientalism Introduction, this week's Hatem lecture, and the section slides. Read ahead for Ch 8, not required here.

    01 · Your TA flagged thisThe likely question

    Likely prompt (your TA said this is probably very important, maybe the question or close to it):

    "In what ways was institutionalization a different strategy, away from assimilation?"

    The move is to define the two against each other and then commit to the contrast.

    Assimilation

    Adapting to the dominant society, reducing religious or cultural difference to gain acceptance. Belonging bought by becoming less visibly Muslim.

    Institutionalization

    Building mosques, schools, and organizations that let Muslims participate in American society while maintaining and passing on their religious identity.

    The one-line answer: institutionalization offered Muslims a way to establish belonging without making the disappearance of their religious difference the price of acceptance. Where earlier Muslims survived by hiding Islam (Ch 4), post-1965 Muslims survived by building it into durable institutions (Ch 7).
    MODELFull model answer for the likely prompt (about 450 words, in a handwriting voice). Adapt the opening to the real wording on the day.
    ~450 words

    Assimilation and institutionalization both answer the same problem, how to survive as a Muslim in America, but they answer it in opposite directions, and that is the whole point. Assimilation buys acceptance by shrinking difference. Institution building buys belonging by preserving it. My argument is that institutionalization was a different strategy because it refused to make the disappearance of Islam the price of being American.

    You see the contrast most clearly by setting GhaneaBassiri's two chapters side by side. In Chapter 4, the Muslims who arrived between 1890 and 1924 survived mostly by erasing themselves. A. Joseph Howar was told that a Muslim name would keep him out, so he passed (around 141). The courts were the real institution deciding who belonged, and in In re Ahmed Hassan a judge ruled that "a wide gulf separates their culture from that of the predominantly Christian peoples of Europe" (158), so Arabs were not white and not admissible. The lesson immigrants drew was to lead with ethnicity and let Islam go quiet. GhaneaBassiri writes that "religious identities became secondary," and that ethnicity "played a greater role than did Islam in shaping their sense of national belonging" (162, 164). That is assimilation as survival, and in practice it meant building Syrian and ethnic clubs, not mosques.

    Chapter 7 is the reversal. After the 1965 Hart-Celler Act, the Muslims who came were post-colonial and confident, "unlikely to willingly change their names" (around 297). Instead of hiding, they built. Mosques multiplied to five times their earlier number, Sunday schools opened, and groups like the MSA set out to produce "Islamic knowledge in its original purity" (305). This is the key difference. The earlier generation survived by becoming less visibly Muslim. This generation survived by turning Islam into something you could teach, fund, and pass to your kids. Institution building let them join American life while keeping, not trading away, their religious identity.

    I want to be honest about the catch, because GhaneaBassiri is. He warns that "greater social and political engagement with American society leads to greater assimilation" (317), and Qureishi feared the system would "assimilate you, and then adopt you, and then change you" (314). So institutions did not escape assimilation completely. But that is a risk inside the strategy, not the strategy itself. The design was different. Assimilation asked Muslims to dissolve into America. Institutionalization asked America to make room for a community that intended to stay itself. That is what makes it a different survival strategy, and a braver one.

    Two more full essays, for the "do they work at the same time" angle and the slide's example prompt, are in section 08.

    03 · OrientalismEdward Said (1978)

    Palestinian-American literary critic at Columbia, born in Jerusalem (1935), exiled in the 1948 Nakba, helped found post-colonial studies. Written after decolonization, the 1973 Arab-Israeli war, and the 1970s oil crises, as US involvement in the Middle East and media hostility to Muslims grew.

    The core quote: "The Orient is an idea that has a history and a tradition of thought, imagery, and vocabulary that have given it reality and presence in and for the West" (12).

    Said's three moves

    • The Orient is a Western construction. It says more about the West than the East. East = irrational, backward, exotic, threatening; West = rational, modern, authoritative.
    • Knowledge is power (Foucault's discourse). Systems of knowledge decide what can be said and accepted as truth. Orientalism becomes "an accepted grid for filtering through the Orient into Western consciousness" (14).
    • Many meanings, one hegemony. "institutions, vocabulary, scholarship, imagery, doctrines, even colonial bureaucracies and colonial styles" (9). "The relationship between Occident and Orient is a relationship of power, of domination, of varying degrees of a complex hegemony" (13, Gramsci).
    Core question (memorize this framing): who has the authority to describe another people, define their culture, and decide what counts as legitimate knowledge?

    Six forms of Orientalism and its afterlives (slides)

    1 Scholarly

    The West as the authoritative interpreter of an East made "fundamentally different."

    2 Aesthetic / cultural

    Exotic, sensual, timeless fantasy (Gerome's Snake Charmer, Rosati's Harem Dance, Arabian Nights).

    3 Political / institutional

    "Incapable of self-government" justifies colonial rule, intervention, surveillance.

    4 Security / threat

    Muslims tied to terrorism; blurs religion, ethnicity, nationality, ideology.

    5 Gendered / rescue

    Muslim women as voiceless, oppressed, needing Western saving (Abu-Lughod, Do Muslim Women Need Saving?).

    6 Everyday / popular

    Headlines, jokes, classrooms. Muslims as perpetual foreigners who must prove loyalty and represent all of Islam.

    Why it matters for Muslims in America: in the US imagination Islam is "not just a religion," it is framed through geopolitical conflict, racial difference, and assumptions about belonging. Representations have material consequences: attitudes, media, immigration debates, security policy.

    04 · Lecture frameworkHatem Bazian

    The seven features of modern colonization ("Erasing the Human")

    Hatem's frame for how colonial power remade the world. It is the structure behind Said: Orientalism is the knowledge arm of this larger project.

    1 Eurocentric epistemology

    European experience made the universal standard of valid knowledge (scientific method, Cartesian individualism, the Western canon, "universal" rights). Other ways of knowing (oral, communal) dismissed.

    2 to 7, the rest of the project

    Global project (85% of the world's surface) · racial project (the line between human and "sub-human," Grosfoguel, Fanon) · capitalism + technology · genocides · demand-driven slavery · Christianity as colonial handmaiden.

    • Said on empire: every empire claims "a mission to enlighten, civilize, bring order and democracy," and says it uses force only as a last resort.
    • Dabashi: inventing "Europe" meant inventing "the white people" as the normative measure of humanity; Europe as white and Christian required expelling Muslims and Jews (Spain, the Inquisition).
    • The link to religion: "the problematic colonial history of the very category of 'religion' challenges the idea that religion exists somehow separately from culture." So Ch 7's "pure vs cultural" Islam is itself a colonial sorting.

    Neoliberal multiculturalism (the bridge concept)

    Institutions celebrate diversity while leaving the underlying structures of inequality and power intact, so you get inclusion without structural change. Muslim visibility is praised through interfaith events, diversity initiatives, and "successful Muslim American" stories, "while Islamophobia, surveillance, and unequal access to institutional power persist." This is how institution building and Orientalism can both be true at once.

    Immigration lecture today: assimilation gets "recoded as a security threat," with "Western values" tests and the Muslim Ban. Exclusion is a structure, not an event: push (war, empire) + pull (capital recruits labor) + limit (the state closes the door by race and religion).

    05 · GhaneaBassiri Ch 4Before 1965: survival = erasure

    "Race, Ethnicity, Religion, and Citizenship: Muslim Immigration at the Turn of the Twentieth Century" (c. 1890 to 1924). The chapter that shows the assimilation strategy institutionalization later broke from.

    Core idea: the conflation of race, religion, and progress defined American identity and restricted entry and citizenship. To belong, immigrants had to copy it, so Muslims claimed belonging through ethnicity, not Islam (137). "ethnicity played a greater role than did Islam in shaping their sense of national belonging" (164).

    How Muslims survived by disappearing

    • Numbers: roughly 10 to 15% of immigrants from Muslim regions 1890 to 1924 were Muslim (about 60,000). Hard to count because of passing and "Turkey in Asia" labels (136). confirm page w/ notes
    • A. Joseph Howar (born Mohammed Asa Abu-Howah): told on the boat that a Muslim name would bar him; took his cousins' names, Abraham and Joseph; naturalized 1908 (141). The textbook case of passing.
    • "De-Islamicization" (141): Ottoman law forbade Muslim emigration, so Muslims passed as Christians or bribed officials, taking Christian names to avoid the stigma of "Turk."
    • Muhammad Sadiq (1920, Ahmadiyya): detained for a faith that allows polygamy; argued it is a permission, not a command, and that he would obey the law of the land (152).

    The court was the institution that mattered

    • Naturalization was for "free white persons" (1790), plus "aliens of African nativity" (1870). Courts decided who was white: Najour 1909 white · Dow 1915 white on appeal · Thind 1923 denied · Hassan 1942 denied · Mohriez 1944 granted.
    • In re Ahmed Hassan (1942), Judge Tuttle: Arabs are "a part of the Mohammedan world and a wide gulf separates their culture from that of the predominantly Christian peoples of Europe" (158). This is Orientalism written into law.
    • Ex parte Mohriez (1944), Judge Wyzanski: "as every schoolboy knows" Arabs carried Greek learning (Avicenna, Averroes, algebra); exclusion is "false to our profession of democratic liberalism" (159).
    • Ethnicity over religion (162): Muslims and Christians united in the Association for Syrian Unity; Christians led, partly because Muslim identity was stigmatized, so "religious identities became secondary."
    Takeaway: before 1965 the institution that produced knowledge about Muslims was the court, and the survival strategy was erasure. Muslims built ethnic, not Islamic, institutions. Institutionalization is what changed after 1965.

    06 · GhaneaBassiri Ch 71960s to 1980s: survival = institutions

    "A New Religious America and a Post-Colonial Muslim World: American Muslim Institution Building and Activism, 1960s to 1980s." The primary reading, and the reversal of Ch 4.

    Foreign born from Muslim-majority countries: 134,615 (1960) → 871,582 (1990). The 1965 Hart-Celler Act abolished the 1924 quotas; Congress did not expect it to "upset the ethnic mix." Diana Eck calls the result "a new religious America."

    The legal and cultural shift

    • The melting pot gave way to pluralism and "identity politics," plus a "white ethnic revival" and "new conservatives" who preferred meritocracy to affirmative action (272 to 281).
    • LBJ, Howard University 1965: "freedom is not enough... you do not take a person who, for years, has been hobbled by chains... and then say, 'you are free to compete'" (279). The logic behind affirmative action.

    The Nation of Islam goes mainstream

    • Once separatism looked like ethnic self-help, the NOI was "no longer seen as a threat"; cities declared Elijah Muhammad days (1973 to 75).
    • Warith Deen Mohammed succeeded Elijah Muhammad (d. 1975), de-mythologized the NOI, dissolved the Fruit of Islam, gave mosques local control, and led followers toward Sunni practice. He called it "reverting," not converting (286). Renamed the movement (World Community of al-Islam in the West, 1976), ran Ethnic Survival Week and New World Patriotism Day, aiming to make Black Muslims "yet another American ethnic community" (288).
    • Farrakhan left in 1977 and rebuilt the old NOI in 1978.

    Institution building (the heart of the reading)

    • Post-1965 immigrants were professionals, post-colonial, with firm identities, "unlikely to willingly change their names" (297). The opposite of Howar.
    • Mosques founded in the 1970s were five times the number of the 1950s or 60s (297); pattern was home → rented or bought house → mosque → Sunday school. A 1994 study found 75% had no paid full-time staff.
    • National groups: MSA, then ISNA (incorporated 1981), NAIT (holds mosque titles, 1973), the Islamic Medical and Social Science associations. They "made themselves available as a resource," they did not impose.
    • MSA's five priorities (1975, 305): "production and dissemination of Islamic knowledge... in its original purity," schools and community centers, daily needs (prayer, Hajj, zakat, marriage, funerals), recruitment, and a unity of Muslim conscience.

    The assimilation debate (direct hit on the prompt)

    • Activists made the Qur'an and Hadith the only "pure" sources to unify a diverse population; GhaneaBassiri notes "there is of course no religion outside of a cultural or social context" (318). Multiculturalism "generally reduced diversity to ethnicity or cultural practices" (316).
    • Enter politics? ISNA ran voter education (1986). Qureishi (NAIT) warned: "The process will assimilate you, and then adopt you, and then change you to its own objective... you will have to make compromises" (314). Note: nobody questioned the fairness of the system itself.
    • Hathout (ICSC): "To be American is not to blindly accept America as it is, but to strive to make it cleaner and better" (317).
    • GhaneaBassiri's own line: "greater social and political engagement with American society leads to greater assimilation" (317).

    Orientalism, live, in the same decades

    • After the 1979 Iranian Revolution and hostage crisis, US discourse shifted from "Arab terrorism" to "Islamic terrorism" (McAlister, 307). "Fundamentalism" (a term from American Protestantism) got pinned on Islam. Neither "Islam" nor "terrorism" was ever defined.
    • ABSCAM: FBI agents posed as rich Arab sheikhs to bribe Congress; FBI surveillance of Muslim and Arab activists since the late 1960s.
    • Double standard: Afghan Islamists backed as "freedom fighters" (Reagan's Afghanistan Day, 1983) while Palestinians were "terrorists." Those fighters later formed al-Qaeda and the Taliban: "categories bent to US interest."
    • End of chapter: "No single national Muslim organization was able to unite Muslims under a single cause or a single understanding of Islam" (326).

    07 · Both at onceHow the two processes run together

    The honest answer to "do they work at the same time" is yes. Orientalism did not end when Muslims started defining themselves; it changed shape and ran through the very institutions meant to resist it.

    • 1908 Howar passes, renamed
    • 1942 Hassan denied ("wide gulf")
    • 1965 Hart-Celler opens the door
    • 1975 MSA "pure" knowledge
    • 1976 W.D. Mohammed, WCIW
    • 1979 Iran; "Islamic terrorism"
    • 1981 ISNA incorporated
    • 2017 Muslim Ban

    08 · Model essaysTwo more worked answers

    The headline model (institutionalization vs assimilation) is in section 01. These cover the other two likely angles. Each commits to one argument and stays in a handwriting voice.

    B"Do Orientalism and the institutionalization of Islamic learning work at the same time?" (about 440 words)
    ~440 words

    Edward Said says the real question behind Orientalism is who has the authority to describe another people and decide what counts as legitimate knowledge. American Muslims tried to answer that question by building institutions. My argument is that institution building and Orientalism are not two separate stories, one about empowerment and one about prejudice. They happen at the same time, and institution building is best understood as a double-edged strategy that answers Orientalism while still being shaped by it.

    GhaneaBassiri shows that after 1965, Muslims built mosques, Sunday schools, and national groups like the MSA and ISNA, mostly through local effort (273 to 274). On its face this reverses Said. Instead of being described by Western scholars, Muslims were producing their own authoritative knowledge about who they were. That is real and it matters. But GhaneaBassiri adds that these loud national organizations "do not represent the majority of the American Muslim population as a whole" (274). That line is where the two processes meet. To become legible to American institutions, a diverse community had to speak in one recognizable voice, and the MSA's goal of teaching Islam "in its original purity" (305) is exactly that move. Said warns that Orientalism works as "an accepted grid for filtering through the Orient into Western consciousness" (14). Producing one "pure" Islam can quietly reproduce that grid from the inside, flattening a plural community into a single essence.

    This is why assimilation is the key. The lecture calls it neoliberal multiculturalism, where diversity is celebrated as an asset but the structures of power stay intact. A thriving MSA or a famous Muslim American gets held up as proof of inclusion, while surveillance and the security-threat image keep running underneath. In the same decades Muslims were building, US discourse shifted to "Islamic terrorism" after 1979 (307), and the FBI ran ABSCAM. Visibility and suspicion grew together.

    So the two processes are not a before and after. Orientalism changed shape and worked through the very institutions meant to resist it. GhaneaBassiri even concedes that "greater social and political engagement with American society leads to greater assimilation" (317). Building Islamic knowledge gave Muslims a voice, but the power to decide how that knowledge gets read stayed with the dominant culture. That is what makes institution building both genuinely powerful and genuinely risky, and it is why the honest answer to whether these processes run together is yes.

    CSlide example prompt: "How might Islamic education engage with broader efforts to celebrate religious diversity while navigating existing social and institutional inequalities?" (about 420 words)
    ~420 words

    Islamic education can absolutely join America's celebration of religious diversity, and GhaneaBassiri's Chapter 7 shows Muslims doing exactly that after 1965, building mosques, Sunday schools, and interfaith-friendly national organizations. My argument is that this engagement is worth it, but under what the lecture calls neoliberal multiculturalism it stays symbolic unless it changes who holds power. Islamic education has to decide whether to be the kind of "diversity" institutions want to display, or to keep teaching an Islam that does not fit the grid.

    Neoliberal multiculturalism celebrates difference as an asset while leaving inequality intact. You get inclusion without structural change. So a Muslim Students Association or a "successful Muslim American" becomes proof that the system is open, even as surveillance and unequal access continue. GhaneaBassiri captures the trap when Qureishi warns that political engagement will "assimilate you, and then adopt you, and then change you to its own objective" (314). The danger is that Islamic education gets invited to the interfaith table precisely because it has already been made legible and unthreatening.

    Said explains why this is more than hurt feelings. Orientalism is "an accepted grid for filtering through the Orient into Western consciousness" (14), so even welcoming institutions read Islam through categories Muslims did not set. When the MSA framed its mission as teaching Islam "in its original purity" (305), it was partly answering that grid and partly reproducing it, by implying one authentic Islam that a university or a diversity office can recognize.

    The way through is to treat Islamic education as more than representation. Hathout's line is the model: "to be American is not to blindly accept America as it is, but to strive to make it cleaner and better" (317). Islamic education engages with diversity most honestly when it does not just supply a comfortable, consumable Islam, but keeps teaching the parts that challenge the structure, including a critique of how knowledge about Muslims gets produced in the first place. Celebrating diversity and naming inequality are not opposites; the first becomes real only when it is willing to do the second.
